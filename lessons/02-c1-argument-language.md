## Words

### contentious
**Definition:** Likely to cause disagreement or argument; people strongly take sides.
**Synonyms:** disputed, divisive, polarising, debatable
**Example:** Housing policy is a contentious issue in almost every big city.
**IPA:** /kənˈtenʃəs/
**Notes:** More precise than “controversial.” It stresses the clash of views, not just public attention.

### ramifications
**Definition:** The extra, often indirect results of a decision or event, especially the serious ones.
**Synonyms:** consequences, knock-on effects, fallout, repercussions
**Example:** They did not think through the ramifications of cutting night buses.
**IPA:** /ˌræmɪfɪˈkeɪʃənz/

### inherent
**Definition:** Built in; a natural part of something, not added from outside.
**Synonyms:** intrinsic, built-in, essential, innate
**Example:** There is an inherent risk in investing all your savings in one company.
**IPA:** /ɪnˈhɪərənt/

### unprecedented
**Definition:** Never known or done before; without an earlier example.
**Synonyms:** unheard of, never before seen, unmatched
**Example:** The heatwave was unprecedented in this region.
**IPA:** /ʌnˈpresɪdentɪd/

### catalyst
**Definition:** Something that sets off or speeds up change, without being the whole cause.
**Synonyms:** trigger, spur, spark, driving force
**Example:** The protest was a catalyst for a wider debate about policing.
**IPA:** /ˈkætəlɪst/
**Notes:** From chemistry: a substance that speeds a reaction. In discussion, it is usually metaphorical.

### detrimental
**Definition:** Causing harm or damage.
**Synonyms:** harmful, damaging, adverse, injurious
**Example:** Sleeping with your phone in bed can be detrimental to rest.
**IPA:** /ˌdetrɪˈmentl/
**Notes:** Often followed by “to”: detrimental to health / to trust.

### credibility
**Definition:** The quality of being believable and worth taking seriously.
**Synonyms:** trustworthiness, authority, plausibility
**Example:** One false statistic can destroy a speaker’s credibility.
**IPA:** /ˌkredəˈbɪləti/

### alike
**Definition:** Used after two groups to mean “both” or “in the same way.”
**Synonyms:** equally, both, similarly
**Example:** The rule frustrated teachers and students alike.
**IPA:** /əˈlaɪk/
**Notes:** Typical pattern: “X and Y alike.”

### harbor
**Definition:** To keep a feeling, thought, or doubt in your mind over time. Also a place where ships shelter.
**Synonyms:** hold, nurse, cling to, harbour
**Example:** She still harbors doubts about the merger.
**IPA:** /ˈhɑːbə/
**Notes:** American spelling: harbor. British: harbour. In arguments it almost always means “hold in the mind,” not a port.

## Phrases

### words and phrases that lend weight and sophistication to your arguments
**Definition:** Language that makes your case sound more serious, precise, and grown-up.
**Synonyms:** language that strengthens your case, wording that sounds more advanced
**Example:** Words like “ramifications” and “inherent” lend weight and sophistication to your arguments.
**Notes:** To “lend weight” is to make something feel more substantial.

### you might find yourself saying things are contentious
**Definition:** A host’s prediction: you will start using the word “contentious” in real talk.
**Synonyms:** you may catch yourself calling things contentious, you might start using that word
**Example:** After this episode, you might find yourself saying things are contentious instead of “a bit spicy.”

### rather than just controversial
**Definition:** A prompt to choose a more exact word than the everyday “controversial.”
**Synonyms:** instead of merely controversial, not simply controversial
**Example:** Call it contentious rather than just controversial, if the point is that people actually fight over it.
**Notes:** “Rather than just X” = a better or more precise choice than the obvious word.

### double-edged sword
**Definition:** Something with both a clear benefit and a clear cost.
**Synonyms:** mixed blessing, two-sided, a plus and a minus
**Example:** Remote work is a double-edged sword: more freedom, but lonelier days.
**Notes:** Standard spelling is hyphenated: double-edged.

### when there are both advantages and drawbacks
**Definition:** A plain way to introduce a balanced view: good points and bad points exist together.
**Synonyms:** when there are pros and cons, when the picture is mixed
**Example:** Use “double-edged sword” when there are both advantages and drawbacks.

### implications of a policy
**Definition:** What a policy is likely to mean in practice; the effects it points to, not just what it says on paper.
**Synonyms:** what a policy would mean, the likely effects of a rule
**Example:** Let’s talk about the implications of a policy that bans cars from the city centre.
**Notes:** “Implications” are often inferred results, not only the official aim.

### perhaps you'll discuss
**Definition:** A soft, speculative way to introduce a possible topic.
**Synonyms:** you might talk about, you may find yourselves discussing
**Example:** Perhaps you’ll discuss whether universities should be free.

### something is … rather than …
**Definition:** A pattern for drawing a contrast: this thing is A, not (merely) B.
**Synonyms:** it is X instead of Y, it’s more A than B
**Example:** The delay was careless rather than malicious.
**Notes:** Fill the gaps: “Something is [more precise word] rather than [weaker everyday word].”

### to play devil's advocate
**Definition:** To argue the opposite view on purpose, to test an idea, not because you fully believe it.
**Synonyms:** to argue the other side, to challenge the idea for the sake of argument
**Example:** Let me play devil’s advocate: what if the ban just pushes the problem elsewhere?
**Notes:** Warn people you are doing this, or they may think it is your real opinion.

### the flip side
**Definition:** The opposite or less obvious side of the same situation.
**Synonyms:** the other side, on the other hand, the downside / the upside
**Example:** The flip side of cheap flights is a much bigger carbon footprint.

### by and large
**Definition:** In general; looking at the whole picture, with small exceptions allowed.
**Synonyms:** on the whole, generally speaking, all in all
**Example:** By and large, the new timetable has worked.
**Notes:** Informal-to-neutral. Good in speech; in a formal essay, “on the whole” is safer.

### to strike a balance
**Definition:** To find a fair middle point between two competing needs.
**Synonyms:** to find a middle ground, to balance X and Y, to weigh both sides
**Example:** Schools have to strike a balance between safety and students’ freedom.

### building blocks of nuanced, compelling discourse
**Definition:** The basic pieces of language that let you talk in a detailed, convincing way.
**Synonyms:** the core tools of careful discussion, the essentials of persuasive talk
**Example:** These expressions are the building blocks of nuanced, compelling discourse.
**Notes:** “Nuanced” = sensitive to small differences. “Compelling” = convincing. “Discourse” = serious discussion, not small talk.

### spot on
**Definition:** Exactly right.
**Synonyms:** exactly right, bang on, dead right, precisely
**Example:** Your point about hidden costs is spot on.
**Notes:** Informal British English. In a formal essay, prefer “precisely right” or “exactly right.”

### navigate complex conversations
**Definition:** To handle difficult, many-sided discussions without getting lost or sounding crude.
**Synonyms:** handle difficult discussions, find your way through tricky talks
**Example:** This vocabulary helps you navigate complex conversations at work.

### casual chat
**Definition:** Relaxed, unofficial talk; small talk rather than a serious debate.
**Synonyms:** informal conversation, a natter, small talk
**Example:** “By and large” is fine in a meeting; it is also natural in casual chat.

### separates … from …
**Definition:** A pattern for saying what makes two things different.
**Synonyms:** distinguishes X from Y, is what sets X apart from Y
**Example:** Careful wording is often what separates a genuinely thought-provoking exchange from a shouty argument.
**Notes:** Fill the gaps: “X separates A from B.”

### genuinely thought-provoking exchange
**Definition:** A real conversation that makes you think harder, not just a polite or noisy one.
**Synonyms:** a truly stimulating discussion, a conversation that actually makes you think
**Example:** I want a genuinely thought-provoking exchange, not a recap of the headlines.
**Notes:** Hyphenate the adjective: thought-provoking.

### dominating headlines
**Definition:** Getting most of the news coverage; being the main story people keep seeing.
**Synonyms:** all over the news, hogging the headlines, in the spotlight
**Example:** The strike has been dominating headlines for a week.

### the impact of …
**Definition:** A stem for talking about the effect something has. Finish it with a noun: the impact of tourism / of AI / of the ban.
**Synonyms:** the effect of, the influence of, what … does to
**Example:** We need to talk about the impact of short-term rentals on local rents.

### friends going head to head on this issue
**Definition:** Friends arguing directly against each other about the same topic.
**Synonyms:** friends clashing over this, friends arguing opposite sides
**Example:** I have two friends going head to head on this issue at every dinner.
**Notes:** “Go head to head” = compete or argue directly, as if in a contest.

### quality of life
**Definition:** How good, healthy, and comfortable everyday living is, not just how much money people have.
**Synonyms:** standard of living, wellbeing, how liveable a place is
**Example:** Clean air is central to quality of life in a city.

### the other harbors some serious reservations
**Definition:** The other person has real, lasting doubts and is not convinced.
**Synonyms:** the other person has strong doubts, the other remains unconvinced
**Example:** One friend is all for the plan; the other harbors some serious reservations.
**Breakdown:** the other = the other person, already contrasted with someone. harbors = holds in the mind over time (not a port). some = more than a tiny doubt. serious = significant, not casual. reservations = doubts or hesitations about agreeing. Whole meaning: unlike the first person, this one is unconvinced and has real concerns.
**Notes:** When a sentence is marked “break this down,” unpack each piece like this instead of only giving a short synonym.
